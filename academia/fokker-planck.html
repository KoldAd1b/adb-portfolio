The Fokker-Planck
Equation

Result

\( \partial_t p_t = -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t) + \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\Delta p_t \)

Method

Test functions, Ito's second-order term, integration by parts.

Connect a diffusion model's random, jittering particles to the smooth density that describes them and you reach for one equation: Fokker-Planck. I use it each time I say the SDE and the distribution \( p_t \) are "the same story," or that the law of the noising process at each instant "is" \( p_t \). You write an SDE for one particle taking one noisy step after another. From Fokker-Planck you get the entire cloud of those particles as a single deterministic flow of probability. The link between the two and the shape of the equation both come from one fact about the roughness of Brownian motion, and I find that mystical.

Setup

Take a particle whose position \( X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d \) evolves according to

\[ dX_t = u_t(X_t)\, dt + \sigma_t\, dW_t, \]

with a deterministic drift \( u_t \) and a random Brownian term \( \sigma_t\, dW_t \). Run this once and you get one jagged trajectory. Run it from many starting points, or many times over, and at each instant \( t \) the particle's position has a distribution; call its density \( p_t(x) \). The drift and the noise give you a rule for the particle. I want the law of motion for the density: a partial differential equation saying how \( p_t \) changes over time. That equation is the target,

\[ \partial_t p_t(x) = -\operatorname{div}\!\big(p_t(x)\, u_t(x)\big) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x), \]

and I derive it by starting from the particle and averaging over many runs.

Test functions

Going straight at \( p_t \) is awkward: you know how to push the particle forward in time, and you have no such rule for its density. I use a classic piece of misdirection instead, and it feels like cheating until the last step. Pick any smooth test function \( f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R} \), treat it as a probe, a measurement you take on the particle, then leave \( p_t \) alone and track how the average reading \( \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] \) changes in time. You get two payoffs. From the particle's motion, you can compute how \( f(X_t) \) changes. And because

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx, \]

anything you learn about the average is a statement about \( p_t \), once you move the derivatives off \( f \) and onto \( p_t \) at the end. You measure the density through every possible probe, then remove the probes.

Taylor expanding one step

Freeze the particle at \( X_t \) and let it take one small step of duration \( h \). Discretize the SDE and the step is

\[ \delta = X_{t+h} - X_t = h\, u_t(X_t) + \sigma_t\, \Delta W, \qquad \Delta W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, h I_d), \]

with the drift contributing a clean \( h\, u_t \) and the noise contributing a Gaussian kick \( \Delta W \) of variance \( h \). The reading changes by \( f(X_t + \delta) - f(X_t) \). You Taylor expand because \( \delta \) is small, and you keep two orders:

\[ f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \approx \nabla f(X_t)^T \delta + \tfrac12\, \delta^T \nabla^2 f(X_t)\, \delta. \]

Substitute \( \delta = h\, u + \sigma_t \Delta W \) (writing \( u = u_t(X_t) \) and \( A = \nabla^2 f(X_t) \) for the Hessian), and you get five pieces on the right-hand side:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \approx\;& h\, \nabla f^T u \\ &+ \sigma_t\, \nabla f^T \Delta W \\ &+ \tfrac12 h^2\, u^T A\, u \\ &+ h \sigma_t\, u^T A\, \Delta W \\ &+ \tfrac12 \sigma_t^2\, \Delta W^T A\, \Delta W. \end{aligned} \]

Now average all five and see which ones remain.

The second-order term

Take the conditional expectation \( \mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid X_t] \): freeze the current position and average only over the fresh noise \( \Delta W \). With \( X_t \) fixed, you can treat \( u \), \( \nabla f \), and \( A \) as constants, and the only random object left is \( \Delta W \). From Brownian motion you have two facts about it,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta W \mid X_t] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\Delta W \Delta W^T \mid X_t] = h I_d. \]

Run down the five terms. The lone-\( \Delta W \) term (second) and the drift-noise cross term (fourth) each carry a single factor of \( \Delta W \), so they average to zero. The drift-drift term (third) is \( O(h^2) \), and you obliterate it when you divide by \( h \) and take the limit. The drift term (first) remains as \( h\, \nabla f^T u \). That leaves the last one, the noise-noise term, and it's the one I care about.

A product of two \( \Delta W \)'s looks negligible, but Brownian motion is confusing: \( \Delta W \) scales like \( \sqrt{h} \) rather than \( h \), so \( \Delta W^2 \) scales like \( h \), the same order as the drift. Make it precise with \( \Delta W = \sqrt{h}\, \varepsilon \), \( \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) \), and the Gaussian quadratic-form identity \( \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^T A \varepsilon] = \operatorname{tr}(A) \):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta W^T \nabla^2 f\, \Delta W \mid X_t] = h\, \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^T \nabla^2 f\, \varepsilon] = h\, \operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 f) = h\, \Delta f, \]

using \( \operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 f) = \Delta f \), since the trace of the Hessian is the Laplacian. A term you would have thrown away remains as the Laplacian, and from it you get the entire diffusion half of Fokker-Planck.

The Ito term

\( \mathbb{E}[\Delta W^T \nabla^2 f\, \Delta W \mid X_t] = h\, \Delta f. \) Brownian motion has \( \sqrt{h} \) roughness, so a second-order term remains as a first-order one, and lo and behold, you have the Laplacian!

Taking the limit

Pick the gold out of the glitter and you have

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \mid X_t] = h\, \nabla f^T u + \tfrac12 h^2\, u^T \nabla^2 f\, u + \frac{h}{2}\, \sigma_t^2\, \Delta f. \]

Divide by \( h \) and let \( h \to 0 \). You lose the \( O(h^2) \) drift-drift term, and the left side becomes the time derivative of the average reading:

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \nabla f(X_t)^T u_t(X_t) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta f(X_t) \right]. \]

You now hold a complete description of the dynamics, told from the particle's side. To turn it into a statement about \( p_t \), write both expectations as integrals against the density,

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int \nabla f(x)^T u_t(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \int \Delta f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx. \]

Every derivative on the right still acts on the probe \( f \). Next, move them to \( p_t \).

Integration by parts

You do this with integration by parts, the powerhouse (mitochondria) of the whole derivation, and you need it for one thing: converting "derivatives of the probe" into "derivatives of the density." For the drift term, apply the identity \( \int \nabla f^T F\, dx = -\int f\, \operatorname{div}(F)\, dx \) with \( F = p_t u_t \) to get

\[ \int \nabla f(x)^T u_t(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx = -\int f(x)\, \operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x)\, dx. \]

The minus sign and the divergence have two sources: integration by parts flips a sign, and the sum of per-coordinate derivatives \( \sum_i \partial_i F_i \) is \( \operatorname{div}(F) \). For the diffusion term, integrate by parts twice (Green's identity), \( \int p_t \Delta f\, dx = \int f \Delta p_t\, dx \):

\[ \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \int \Delta f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx = \int f(x)\, \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x)\, dx. \]

I slipped an assumption into both steps: the density and its gradient decay at infinity, so the boundary terms vanish. That holds for any distribution worth the name. Put the two together and the whole right-hand side is a single integral with every derivative acting on \( p_t \):

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int f(x) \left[ -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x) \right] dx. \]

Recovering the PDE

You have one thread left. The left side, \( \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] \), is also \( \partial_t \int f\, p_t\, dx = \int f\, \partial_t p_t\, dx \), since \( f \) does not depend on time. So

\[ \int f(x)\, \partial_t p_t(x)\, dx = \int f(x) \left[ -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x) \right] dx, \]

and this holds for every smooth test function \( f \). Two functions that agree when you integrate them against every test function must equal each other pointwise. Remove the probes and you are left with the equation:

Fokker-Planck

\( \partial_t p_t(x) = -\operatorname{div}\!\big(p_t(x)\, u_t(x)\big) + \dfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x). \)

Interpreting the equation

Read the two terms. The first, \( -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t) \), is transport: the drift \( u_t \) carries probability mass around, the same continuity equation you write for a flowing fluid. The second, \( \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \Delta p_t \), is diffusion: the Laplacian, which comes from Brownian roughness, smooths and spreads the density, flattening peaks and filling valleys. Density change is transport by drift plus diffusion by noise, and you now know where each half came from.

I call this the support vector of diffusion. I need it for the claim I make all over the five views, that the SDE's law at every instant is the distribution \( p_t \): you have one model, and this PDE is the deterministic description of the particle's random walk at the level of the density. It is also your doorway to the probability-flow ODE, where you take this same equation, fold the diffusion term into the transport term with \( \Delta p_t = \operatorname{div}(p_t \nabla \log p_t) \), and rewrite the noise term as a velocity. (Map the names back: there you will see the drift \( u_t \) written as \( f \), the noise \( \sigma_t \) as \( g \), and \( \Delta \) as \( \nabla^2 \).)

The best part of discovering this equation, for me, is that Planck gets to be popular for something besides the Planck constant...
