The Fokker-Planck
Equation

Result

\( \partial_t p_t = -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t) + \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\Delta p_t \)

Method

Test functions, Ito's second-order term, integration by parts.

Connect a diffusion model's random, jittering particles to the smooth density they belong to and you reach for one equation: Fokker-Planck. I have used it more than once, each time I said the SDE and the distribution \( p_t \) are "the same story," or that the law of the noising process at each instant "is" \( p_t \). You write an SDE for one particle taking one noisy step after another, all jitter and randomness. Fokker-Planck hands you the entire cloud of those particles as a single deterministic flow of probability, smooth and lawful. The bridge from one to the other is a single surprising fact about how rough Brownian motion is. That one fact is the whole reason the equation has the shape it does, and I find that mystical.

One particle, and the crowd it belongs to

Take a particle whose position \( X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d \) wanders according to

\[ dX_t = u_t(X_t)\, dt + \sigma_t\, dW_t, \]

with a deterministic drift \( u_t \) nudging it along and a Brownian term \( \sigma_t\, dW_t \) shoving it around at random. Run this once and you get one jagged trajectory. Run it from many starting points, or many times over, and at each instant \( t \) the particle's position has a distribution; call its density \( p_t(x) \). The drift and the noise give you a rule for the particle. I want the law of motion for the density: a partial differential equation saying how \( p_t \) sloshes and spreads over time. That equation is the target,

\[ \partial_t p_t(x) = -\operatorname{div}\!\big(p_t(x)\, u_t(x)\big) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x), \]

and I know one honest way to understand it: start from the particle and let the crowd form.

Inspect the density with a probe

Going straight at \( p_t \) is awkward: you know how to push the particle forward in time, and you have no such rule for its density. I use a classic piece of misdirection instead, and it feels like cheating right up until you see why. Pick any smooth test function \( f : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R} \), treat it as a probe, a measurement you take on the particle, then leave \( p_t \) alone and track how the average reading \( \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] \) changes in time. You get two payoffs. From the particle's motion, you can compute how \( f(X_t) \) changes. And because

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx, \]

anything you learn about the average is a statement about \( p_t \) in disguise, once you shuffle the derivatives off \( f \) and onto \( p_t \) at the end. You interrogate the density through every possible probe, then let the probes go.

A single step, Taylor expanded

Freeze the particle at \( X_t \) and let it take one small step of duration \( h \). Discretize the SDE and the step is

\[ \delta = X_{t+h} - X_t = h\, u_t(X_t) + \sigma_t\, \Delta W, \qquad \Delta W \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, h I_d), \]

with the drift contributing a clean \( h\, u_t \) and the noise contributing a Gaussian kick \( \Delta W \) of variance \( h \). The reading changes by \( f(X_t + \delta) - f(X_t) \). You Taylor expand because \( \delta \) is small, and you keep two orders:

\[ f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \approx \nabla f(X_t)^T \delta + \tfrac12\, \delta^T \nabla^2 f(X_t)\, \delta. \]

Substitute \( \delta = h\, u + \sigma_t \Delta W \) (writing \( u = u_t(X_t) \) and \( A = \nabla^2 f(X_t) \) for the Hessian), and you get five pieces on the right-hand side:

\[ \begin{aligned} f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \approx\;& h\, \nabla f^T u \\ &+ \sigma_t\, \nabla f^T \Delta W \\ &+ \tfrac12 h^2\, u^T A\, u \\ &+ h \sigma_t\, u^T A\, \Delta W \\ &+ \tfrac12 \sigma_t^2\, \Delta W^T A\, \Delta W. \end{aligned} \]

Now average all five and watch which ones survive.

The term you would throw away

Take the conditional expectation \( \mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid X_t] \): freeze the current position and average only over the fresh noise \( \Delta W \). With \( X_t \) fixed, you can treat \( u \), \( \nabla f \), and \( A \) as constants, and the only random object left is \( \Delta W \). From Brownian motion you have two facts about it,

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta W \mid X_t] = 0, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\Delta W \Delta W^T \mid X_t] = h I_d. \]

Run down the five terms. The lone-\( \Delta W \) term (second) and the drift-noise cross term (fourth) each carry a single factor of \( \Delta W \), so they average to zero. The drift-drift term (third) is \( O(h^2) \), and you obliterate it when you divide by \( h \) and take the limit. The drift term (first) survives as \( h\, \nabla f^T u \). That leaves the last one, the noise-noise term, and it's the one I care about.

You would expect a product of two \( \Delta W \)'s to be negligible, second order, gone, and you would be wrong, because Brownian motion is confusing: \( \Delta W \) scales like \( \sqrt{h} \) rather than \( h \), so \( \Delta W^2 \) scales like \( h \), the same order as the drift. Make it precise with \( \Delta W = \sqrt{h}\, \varepsilon \), \( \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d) \), and the Gaussian quadratic-form identity \( \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^T A \varepsilon] = \operatorname{tr}(A) \):

\[ \mathbb{E}[\Delta W^T \nabla^2 f\, \Delta W \mid X_t] = h\, \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^T \nabla^2 f\, \varepsilon] = h\, \operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 f) = h\, \Delta f, \]

using \( \operatorname{tr}(\nabla^2 f) = \Delta f \), since the trace of the Hessian is the Laplacian. Stop here for a second: a term you would have thrown away survives, and it survives as the Laplacian. You get the entire diffusion half of Fokker-Planck right here, out of a quantity you almost discarded.

The Ito term

\( \mathbb{E}[\Delta W^T \nabla^2 f\, \Delta W \mid X_t] = h\, \Delta f. \) Brownian motion has \( \sqrt{h} \) roughness, so a second-order term survives as a first-order one, and lo and behold, you have the Laplacian!

From the particle to the density

Pick the gold out of the glitter and you have

\[ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{t+h}) - f(X_t) \mid X_t] = h\, \nabla f^T u + \tfrac12 h^2\, u^T \nabla^2 f\, u + \frac{h}{2}\, \sigma_t^2\, \Delta f. \]

Divide by \( h \) and let \( h \to 0 \). You lose the \( O(h^2) \) drift-drift term, and the left side becomes the time derivative of the average reading:

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \nabla f(X_t)^T u_t(X_t) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta f(X_t) \right]. \]

Here you already hold a complete description of the dynamics, told from the particle's side. To turn it into a statement about \( p_t \), write both expectations as integrals against the density,

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int \nabla f(x)^T u_t(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \int \Delta f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx. \]

Every derivative on the right still sits on the probe \( f \). Next, hand them back to \( p_t \).

Hand the derivatives back to the density

You do this with integration by parts, the powerhouse (mitochondria) of the whole derivation, and you need it for one thing: converting "derivatives of the probe" into "derivatives of the density." For the drift term, apply the identity \( \int \nabla f^T F\, dx = -\int f\, \operatorname{div}(F)\, dx \) with \( F = p_t u_t \) to get

\[ \int \nabla f(x)^T u_t(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx = -\int f(x)\, \operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x)\, dx. \]

The minus sign and the divergence stare at you for a reason: integration by parts flips a sign, and the sum of per-coordinate derivatives \( \sum_i \partial_i F_i \) is \( \operatorname{div}(F) \). For the diffusion term, integrate by parts twice (Green's identity), \( \int p_t \Delta f\, dx = \int f \Delta p_t\, dx \):

\[ \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \int \Delta f(x)\, p_t(x)\, dx = \int f(x)\, \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x)\, dx. \]

I slipped an assumption into both steps: the density and its gradient decay at infinity, so the boundary terms vanish. That holds for any distribution worth the name. Put the two together and the whole right-hand side is a single integral with every derivative resting on \( p_t \):

\[ \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] = \int f(x) \left[ -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x) \right] dx. \]

Strip the integral and read off the PDE

You have one thread left. The left side, \( \partial_t \mathbb{E}[f(X_t)] \), is also \( \partial_t \int f\, p_t\, dx = \int f\, \partial_t p_t\, dx \), since \( f \) does not depend on time. So

\[ \int f(x)\, \partial_t p_t(x)\, dx = \int f(x) \left[ -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t)(x) + \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x) \right] dx, \]

and this holds for every smooth test function \( f \). Two functions that agree when you integrate them against every test function must equal each other pointwise. The probes have done their job. Let them go, and you are left with the equation:

Fokker-Planck

\( \partial_t p_t(x) = -\operatorname{div}\!\big(p_t(x)\, u_t(x)\big) + \dfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\, \Delta p_t(x). \)

Reading the equation, and why I needed it

Read the two terms. The first, \( -\operatorname{div}(p_t u_t) \), is transport: the drift \( u_t \) carries probability mass around, the same continuity equation you write for a flowing fluid. The second, \( \tfrac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \Delta p_t \), is diffusion: the Laplacian, that present from Brownian roughness, smooths and spreads the density, flattening peaks and filling valleys. Density change is transport by drift plus diffusion by noise, and you now know where each half came from.

I call this the support vector the rest of diffusion swings on. I need it for the claim I make all over the five views, that the SDE's law at every instant is the distribution \( p_t \): you have one model, and the density is the deterministic shadow this PDE casts from the particle's random walk. It is also your doorway to the probability-flow ODE, where you take this same equation, fold the diffusion term into the transport term with \( \Delta p_t = \operatorname{div}(p_t \nabla \log p_t) \), and watch the noise turn into a velocity. (Map the names back: there you will see the drift \( u_t \) written as \( f \), the noise \( \sigma_t \) as \( g \), and \( \Delta \) as \( \nabla^2 \).)

The best part of discovering this equation, for me, is that Planck gets to be popular for something besides the Planck constant...
